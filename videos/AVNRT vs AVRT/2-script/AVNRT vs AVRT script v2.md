## Narration v2

### 1. Cold open
Two patients. Both have a sudden, fast, narrow, regular rhythm.
On the monitor, they look almost the same. But the loops driving them are very different sizes.
One fits in and around the AV node, a structure about five millimeters long. **The other is a much bigger circuit: through the atria, the ventricles, and back.**

### 2. What a loop needs
Normally, the atria and ventricles are electrically insulated from each other by fibrous rings around the valves. **The only normal connection runs through the AV node and the His bundle.**
A re-entry loop needs two pathways that are joined at both ends, but behave differently. One conducts fast. The other conducts slowly. And they take different amounts of time to recover.
Then it needs a trigger, usually a premature beat that arrives at just the wrong moment.

### 3. AVNRT: the small loop
**In typical A V N R T, both pathways sit in and around the AV node.**
The fast pathway conducts quickly, but takes a long time to recover. The slow pathway conducts slowly, but recovers quickly.
Normal beats travel down the fast pathway. But a premature beat can arrive while the fast pathway is still recovering. So it travels down the slow pathway instead.
By the time it reaches the bottom, the fast pathway has recovered. The signal turns and races back up it, then down the slow pathway again. The loop is now self-sustaining.
Every lap fires the ventricles and the atria at almost the same instant. So the P wave lands inside the QRS, or just at its end. Look for a small pseudo S wave in leads two, three, and A V F, or a pseudo R prime in V one.
A V N R T is the most common **paroxysmal** S V T in adults, about half to sixty percent of cases. It usually shows up in young adults, and about two thirds are women.

### 4. AVRT: the big loop
A V R T needs an accessory pathway: a strand of muscle that crosses the insulating ring between the atria and the ventricles.
In the usual form, called orthodromic, the signal goes down the AV node and the His-Purkinje system, so the QRS is **usually** narrow. Then it crosses back up the accessory pathway to the atria, and returns to the AV node. **That's the large majority of cases.**
This loop is much longer. The atria fire noticeably later than the ventricles. So the P wave often shows up after the QRS, in the S T segment. **The R P interval is longer than in typical A V N R T, often more than seventy milliseconds.**
Less often, the loop runs the other way. Down the accessory pathway, and back up the node. This is antidromic A V R T, and because the ventricles are activated through the pathway instead of the fast His-Purkinje system, the QRS is wide.
A V R T is the most common S V T in children, **especially younger children.**

### 5. The resting ECG
Between episodes, the resting E C G can carry a clue.
Some accessory pathways also conduct forward during normal rhythm. They let the ventricles start early. **That shortens the P R interval, adds a slurred upstroke called a delta wave, and widens the QRS.** That's the Wolff Parkinson White pattern.
Other pathways only conduct backward. They're concealed, **and show no sign of pre-excitation on the resting E C G.** So a normal resting E C G does not rule out A V R T.

### 6. Breaking the loop, and curing it
Here's what the two loops have in common. In both A V N R T and A V R T, the AV node is usually part of the circuit.
**So for a stable patient with a regular, narrow-complex tachycardia, the first steps are the same. Try a vagal maneuver, like Valsalva. If that fails, give adenosine, unless it's contraindicated.** Blocking the AV node for a moment breaks either loop. **If the tachycardia is making the patient unstable, use synchronized cardioversion.**
**Wide-complex rhythms need more caution.** In antidromic A V R T, avoid AV nodal blockers. Procainamide is an alternative.
**And watch for pre-excited atrial fibrillation: a very fast, irregular, wide-complex rhythm in a patient with an accessory pathway. It can be lethal. AV nodal blockers, including adenosine, can make it worse. Stable patients can get procainamide. Unstable patients need electrical cardioversion.**
For a cure, catheter ablation targets the loop itself. In A V N R T, that's the slow pathway, with success above ninety-five percent. In A V R T, it's the accessory pathway.

### 7. Close
Same speed. Different size.
**A small loop usually hides the P wave inside the QRS. A big loop usually leaves it trailing behind. It's a clue, not a rule.**
