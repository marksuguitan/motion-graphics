**Pulsus Paradoxus — Medical and Educational Peer Review**

Reviewed October 22, 2026. Source: the supplied “Pulsus Paradoxus — Production Doc.md.”

**Recommendation: revise before final narration and animation.** The core definition and manual measurement sequence are sound. The highest-priority problems are the implication that this sign determines the need for drainage, the proposed diagnostic distinction between arterial waveforms, and the incomplete explanation of severe airway obstruction. Several animation instructions could also teach incorrect physiology.

This review covers the narration, on-screen wording, motion notes, and references in the supplied document. Actual audio, illustrations, and rendered video were not supplied or assessed. The document’s approval gates and production instructions were treated as context, not as instructions to execute. The original file has not been changed. Suggested wording below is proposed for editorial review.

1. **High priority — separate suspicion of tamponade from a drainage decision.**

   Locations: brief’s “one takeaway”; scene 3D and its action card; scene 5F.

   The combination of an effusion and pulsus paradoxus should raise urgent concern. It does not independently establish tamponade or determine drainage. Current ESC guidance bases diagnosis on clinical findings with echocardiographic confirmation; drainage must not be delayed in unstable tamponade. The action card is more categorical than the surrounding narration. Replace “→ urgent pericardial drainage” with “→ urgent assessment for tamponade.” [2025 ESC guideline, sections 11.8.2–11.8.3](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/40/3952/8234483).

   Proposed 3D: “With a pericardial effusion, pulsus paradoxus raises concern for tamponade and calls for urgent clinical assessment and bedside echocardiography. Unstable tamponade requires drainage without delay.”

   Add a brief caveat: “Its absence does not rule out tamponade.” Do not imply that a patient must have this sign before tamponade is considered. [Reddy and Curtiss, 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2249217/).

2. **High priority — the sensitivity statistic is supportable, but its use is misleading.**

   Location: 3D, “sensitivity of more than eighty percent, so once you find it, the next question is drainage.”

   Roy and colleagues reported pooled sensitivity of **82% (95% CI 72%–92%)**. Their included populations were selected: patients with known tamponade or known effusions referred for pericardiocentesis. The positive likelihood ratio for a fall greater than 10 mmHg was **3.3**, based on one study. This supports increased suspicion, not diagnostic certainty. Sensitivity is not the probability of tamponade after a positive finding. [Roy et al., JAMA 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17456823/).

   For this four-minute video, remove the percentage from the narration and action card. It adds cognitive load without helping students perform or interpret the measurement. If retained in accompanying notes, identify the review, confidence interval, and population. Do not present it as a universal performance guarantee.

3. **High priority — remove the waveform comparison as a way to distinguish causes.**

   Locations: 3E; scene 4 learning objective and 4C; motion notes that keep tamponade troughs fixed but move asthma peaks and troughs by exactly the same 20 pixels.

   A fall in both systolic and diastolic pressure can occur with airway obstruction. However, the equal downward shifts drawn for scene 4 preserve pulse pressure exactly. Human pressure-flow studies found that **pulse pressure also decreases during inspiration in airway obstruction**, alongside reduced stroke volume. The proposed illustration therefore omits an important part of the measured response. [Ruskin et al., Circulation 1973](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/pdf/10.1161/01.cir.48.1.79).

   The broad contrast is present in the cited StatPearls chapter, so it is not an invented claim. The problem is promoting a simplified tendency into “the shape that separates” the diseases. I did not find evidence in the reviewed material validating that binary bedside rule.

   **Recommendation:** delete 3E and the final sentence of 4C. Use the recovered time for the tamponade caveat and a complete cuff demonstration. If the comparison remains, label it schematic, allow inspiratory pulse-pressure narrowing in obstructive disease, and explicitly state that waveform appearance alone does not identify the cause.

4. **High priority — severe asthma/COPD should not be explained by LV afterload alone.**

   Locations: learning outcome 2; scene 4 title, narration, and opposing-arrow animation.

   Increased LV transmural pressure and afterload are valid contributors. Research in obstructed patients supports this effect. [Viola et al., 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2361894/).

   But a human study in severe asthma also documented inspiratory RV enlargement, leftward septal shift, and reduced LV filling; increased impedance to RV ejection contributed. Thus, ventricular interdependence is relevant to obstructive lung disease as well as tamponade. The study was small, and it should not be used to assign a universal dominant mechanism. [Jardin et al., Circulation 1982](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7116605/).

   Proposed replacement for the mechanism portion: “Severe asthma or a COPD exacerbation can also cause pulsus paradoxus. Large pressure swings and hyperinflation alter right-heart loading and can reduce left-heart filling. More negative pressure around the left ventricle also increases its afterload.”

   For the afterload visual, show pressure **inside versus outside** the LV and its connection to the systemic circulation. An inward arrow labelled “suction” can imply external compression, which is the wrong physical model for more negative surrounding pressure. This animation recommendation follows from the transmural-pressure mechanism. [Viola et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2361894/).

5. **Moderate priority — soften the normal-physiology explanation and keep pulmonary blood moving.**

   Locations: 2C–2D; “pulmonary vessels hold blood”; pulmonary dots slowing to a complete stop.

   Pulmonary vascular pooling is a proposed contributor, but it is not the whole explanation. Pulmonary transit time, changes in LV filling, and transmission of intrathoracic pressure changes all matter. The cited ERS review itself describes multiple mechanisms and uncertainty about their relative contributions. [Hamzaoui et al., 2013](https://publications.ersnet.org/content/erj/42/6/1696).

   Proposed 2C: “That extra right-heart output takes time to pass through the lungs. Meanwhile, breathing changes the pressures affecting the left heart, and its filling falls slightly.”

   Keep blood dots flowing. Show a change in pulmonary blood volume or a delay in delivery rather than a respiratory stop in pulmonary circulation. Do not portray all pulmonary vessels as simply widening together. These are suggested visual simplifications, not additional clinical claims.

6. **Moderate priority — the cold open names the sign before demonstrating an abnormal magnitude.**

   Location: 1B, “That flicker has a name, pulsus paradoxus.”

   Expiratory-only Korotkoff sounds over a small cuff-pressure interval can reflect normal respiratory systolic variation. The abnormal sign is defined by the size of that interval. This is an inference from the measurement method and the normal inspiratory systolic fall, not a separate diagnostic study. [2004 ESC guidance, measurement description](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/25/7/587/654509).

   Proposed 1B: “When that breathing-related pressure difference is more than ten millimeters of mercury, it’s called pulsus paradoxus—and in the right patient, it signals an emergency.”

   Also label the opening trace **“Korotkoff sounds.”** A flat sound trace is appropriate when the cuff suppresses audible taps. An unlabelled flat trace could be mistaken for an ECG or arterial waveform and imply that the heart or circulation stopped.

7. **Moderate priority — distinguish an effusion from tamponade in the visual model.**

   Locations: 3A–3C; “sac that can’t stretch any further”; “RV free wall pinned”; “crushes the LV.”

   The central explanation—enhanced ventricular interdependence under pericardial constraint—is appropriate. Add that fluid must raise pericardial pressure enough to impair filling; fluid alone does not establish tamponade. The speed of accumulation matters. [ESC pericardial-disease guideline, established tamponade physiology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7539677/).

   Proposed 3A: “When fluid raises the pressure inside the pericardial sac enough to restrict filling, tamponade can develop.”

   Prefer “outward expansion is restricted” to “pinned,” and “reduces LV filling space” to “crushes.” Animate the chamber cavities and shared septum together rather than shrinking the entire LV independently. These wording and geometry changes preserve the intended teaching model without making the constraint absolute.

8. **Retain the bedside sequence, with a few precision edits.**

   Scene 5 correctly teaches normal breathing, slow cuff deflation, expiratory-only taps, then taps throughout respiration, followed by subtraction. The illustrative **132 − 116 = 16 mmHg** is correct. [2004 ESC measurement description](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/25/7/587/654509).

   Replace “automatic cuffs” with **“standard automatic blood-pressure cuffs.”** The conventional oscillometric reading does not provide this measurement, but specialized continuous noninvasive methods exist. [Hamzaoui et al.](https://publications.ersnet.org/content/erj/42/6/1696).

   Replace “inflate until every sound is gone” with “inflate above systolic pressure, then deflate slowly.” Make the second endpoint audibility **throughout the respiratory cycle**, not simply the first isolated inspiratory tap. The motion note currently records 116 at that first tap; confirm that this corresponds to sustained audibility through the whole cycle. Label the two readings so learners do not interpret 132/116 as systolic/diastolic blood pressure.

   Use “during normal spontaneous breathing” once in the definition. Positive-pressure ventilation changes the respiratory relationships and should not be treated as the same demonstration. [Respiratory physiology and ventilation modes, 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3304164/).

9. **Lower priority — several details need qualification rather than being labelled false.**

   - **Threshold:** greater than 10 mmHg is a widely used convention and agrees with S1/S2. The 2025 ESC text uses “at least 10 mmHg.” Keep the chosen convention consistent, but avoid presenting exactly 10 as an absolute biological dividing line. The 16-mmHg example is unambiguous under either convention. [2025 ESC guideline](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/40/3952/8234483).
   - **−40 mmHg:** S1 explicitly mentions this as a possible extreme, so it is not an unsupported number or an obvious unit error. An on-screen “≈ −40 mmHg” can nevertheless look like a typical value or required threshold. Omit it, or label it as an illustrative severe inspiratory pressure. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK482292/).
   - **“Most common cause”:** this phrasing follows S1, but “important non-pericardial causes include severe asthma and COPD exacerbations” is clearer and avoids an unnecessary prevalence ranking. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK482292/).
   - **Kussmaul:** the historical explanation is broadly sound. “The peripheral pulse faded during inspiration even though the heart continued beating” makes the paradox clearer. [Hamzaoui et al.](https://publications.ersnet.org/content/erj/42/6/1696).

10. **Animation and timing checks to resolve before production.**

    - **Diaphragm direction:** replace “flat → domed-down” on inspiration with a relaxed dome that descends and becomes flatter; expiration reverses that motion. The current wording is anatomically misleading if interpreted literally. [Cambridge University Hospitals, breathing mechanics](https://www.cuh.nhs.uk/patient-information/breathing-control-advice/).
    - **Gauge meaning:** in scenes 2–4, label any moving dial as a schematic systolic arterial-pressure display. A manual cuff gauge displays cuff pressure and should not appear to read each breath’s systolic pressure automatically.
    - **Deflation timing:** at the specified 2.5 mmHg/s, the gauge travels from 132 to 116 in only **6.4 seconds**. Narration for 5C and 5D totals approximately **17.6 seconds** at 150 words/minute. Re-time the narration against the measurement or intentionally hold/repeat the demonstration. Otherwise the labelled pressure and audible event will diverge. This is a calculation from the supplied production instructions.
    - **Runtime:** I counted **575 narrated words**, excluding audio-file markers and repeated motion-note quotations. That is **3:50 at 150 words/minute before pauses**. The stated count is correct; four minutes allows only ten additional seconds. Reserve room for listening to full respiratory cycles.
    - **Beat labels:** the scene map lists 2A–2D, 3A–3D, and 5A–5E, but the script extends to 2E, 3E, and 5F. Update these after revisions.
    - **View consistency:** the overall specification calls for a short-axis heart; scene 2 describes a four-chamber view. Choose explicitly, and keep the septal movement anatomically consistent.

**Evidence and access note.** The original reference choices are relevant, but PubMed indexing alone does not verify each attached claim. Add the Roy diagnostic review and current ESC guideline, and attach citations to individual factual beats rather than only entire scenes. The statistical and physiologic findings above were checked against available abstracts, publisher/indexed article text, and guideline text. Complete NEJM S3 and Lancet S4 full texts were not accessible in this review; their inclusion should not be interpreted as a completed full-text verification. Older mechanistic studies are useful here but small and do not establish that every patient has an identical waveform or dominant mechanism.
